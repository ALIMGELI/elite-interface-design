# Design Directions

Use these as starting points, not fixed templates. Combine carefully when the product benefits from it.

## A. Refined Minimal / Premium Product
**Good for:** professional SaaS, productivity, financial tools, focused workflows.
**Traits:** restrained palette, crisp typography, intentional whitespace, precise borders, clear interaction states.
**Risks:** can become bland or overly sparse if product needs high data density.

## B. Editorial / Swiss Grid
**Good for:** analytics storytelling, content-heavy products, creative tools, premium landing pages.
**Traits:** strong typographic hierarchy, disciplined grid, asymmetry with purpose, clear reading order.
**Risks:** decorative layouts can impair task speed in operational software.

## C. Data-Dense Enterprise
**Good for:** ERP, accounting, inventory, operations, admin consoles.
**Traits:** compact but readable tables, clear filters, sticky headers when useful, strong number alignment, status chips, fast keyboard workflows.
**Risks:** avoid turning every data point into a card or shrinking type excessively.

## D. Expressive / Bold
**Good for:** consumer products, creative platforms, launch experiences, distinctive brands.
**Traits:** memorable composition, selective bold color, confident typography, strong focal points.
**Risks:** visual drama must not obscure actions or accessibility.

## E. Spatial / Futuristic
**Good for:** AI tools, advanced analytics, simulation, technology brands.
**Traits:** depth, layered surfaces, focused glow or gradients, precise motion, technical typography.
**Risks:** avoid low-contrast text, excessive blur, neon overload, and decorative controls.

## F. Warm / Human-Centered
**Good for:** education, healthcare-adjacent non-clinical tools, collaboration, community products.
**Traits:** warm neutrals, approachable language, clear illustrations, gentle interaction.
**Risks:** keep professional data and actions precise.

## G. Editorial Commerce
**Good for:** retail, catalogs, booking, product discovery.
**Traits:** high-quality imagery, clear price hierarchy, filters, concise product information, coherent product cards.
**Risks:** do not let imagery hide comparison data or accessibility needs.

## Selection procedure
1. List the product's top three user tasks.
2. Identify brand tone and required information density.
3. Pick one primary direction and, if useful, one supporting influence.
4. Explain why it fits and what trade-offs it introduces.
5. Define concrete tokens and layout choices before implementation.
6. If the user asks for a direct build, choose a sensible direction and proceed rather than blocking on approval.
