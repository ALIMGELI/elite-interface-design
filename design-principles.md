# Design Principles

## 1. Visual hierarchy
- Make the primary task and most important information obvious at first glance.
- Use size, weight, contrast, placement, spacing, and grouping together; do not rely on color alone.
- Establish a clear order: page purpose, key status or summary, primary action, supporting details, secondary actions.
- Keep headings descriptive and concise.

## 2. Composition and rhythm
- Use a deliberate grid and consistent alignment.
- Vary density intentionally: dense data areas may need compact spacing, while onboarding or marketing pages may need more breathing room.
- Avoid filling every gap with cards. Use typography, dividers, background regions, and whitespace where appropriate.
- Keep related elements together and separate unrelated tasks.

## 3. Color
- Assign semantic roles: canvas, surface, raised surface, text, muted text, border, primary, accent, success, warning, danger, info.
- Check contrast for text and meaningful controls.
- Use accent colors sparingly enough to preserve meaning.
- Use gradients only when they support the product's identity or hierarchy.

## 4. Typography
- Select typefaces appropriate to the language and platform. Ensure Arabic glyph quality when designing Arabic products.
- Define a type scale and consistent line heights.
- Avoid tiny secondary text and excessively light font weights.
- Use tabular numerals for financial figures and aligned numerical columns when supported.
- Do not use typography as decoration at the expense of legibility.

## 5. Surfaces and depth
- Use borders, elevation, and surface contrast to clarify layers.
- Avoid stacking shadows, blur, transparency, and borders without a clear hierarchy.
- Ensure controls remain legible in light and dark themes.
- Do not make every section float independently.

## 6. Interaction
- Every interactive element should have a clear affordance and visible states: default, hover, focus, pressed, disabled, loading, selected, and error as applicable.
- Keep destructive actions visually distinct and provide confirmation when consequences are significant.
- Use feedback close to the action that caused it.
- Avoid surprising motion or transitions that slow down frequent work.

## 7. Content and realism
- Use domain-realistic labels, values, dates, and sample records.
- Write action labels as verbs: “Create invoice”, “Save changes”, “Export report”.
- Design for long names, missing values, errors, and large data sets.
- Never use dummy controls that look functional but do nothing.

## 8. Originality
- Start from user tasks, product constraints, and brand personality.
- Create a coherent point of view through layout, type, rhythm, color, and interaction—not a pile of trendy effects.
- Use inspiration as a reference for principles, then produce a distinct solution.
